Intentional, meaningful philanthropy: Bridging strategy and humility

Rather than over-emphasizing short-term gains and measurable outputs over capacity building and shared values, here’s how we can be more collaborative and make intentional, meaningful philanthropic impact.

October 29, 2026 By Hanna Shaul Bar Nissim, PhD

At a time when nonprofits are facing increasing demand for services, growing uncertainty around funding, and heightened pressure to demonstrate results, the conversation about how philanthropy measures success has become more important than ever. Organizations increasingly emphasize “strategy,” “metrics,” and “impact” because that is the language funders have adopted.

As the sector has adopted more evidence-based and data-driven practices, two important questions have emerged: How does this shift affect the relationship between nonprofits and their funders? And how can nonprofits ensure growing expectations around measurement don’t impede their ability to achieve their missions?

U.S. charitable giving totaled over $617 billion in 2025 according to Giving USA. That includes over $117 billion from foundations and nearly $44 billion from corporations, a scale and scope of institutional philanthropy that adds to the urgency of those two questions.

Philanthropy risks becoming more transactional

Entrepreneurial, data-driven grantmaking have deeply embedded terms such as “capitalizing,” “maximizing,” “investing,” “measuring,” “assessing,” and “scaling” into philanthropic practice. Funders are crafting their practices to align with their chosen identities as conveners, experts, incubators, drivers of change, activists, and impact investors.

Often described as philanthrocapitalism or the financialization of philanthropy, this approach has resulted in a greater emphasis on controlling the grantmaking process and the roles nonprofits have within it. At times, it can lead to a more transactional form of philanthropy that prioritizes short-term gains over long-term capacity building and measurable outputs over shared values.

Nonprofits can encourage collaborative philanthropy

This shift in the perception of philanthropy has pressured nonprofits to adapt their behavior and relationships with funders. When traditional power relations are replaced by an evidence-based and scientific approach, it should lead to more equitable partnerships and effective, impactful grantmaking. Yet it often falls short. Instead, the personal and experiential is replaced by performance-based models and projections of fiscal efficiency and desired impact. These, in turn, fail to capture the accountability funders’ share in the success of the partnership and places it all on the nonprofit.

The shift toward emphasizing short-term gains and measurable outputs over capacity building and shared values can limit both funders’ and grantees’ ability to advance their missions. Funders can take a decision-making process that clarifies priorities and goals. It’s time to rebuild trust-based relationships with nonprofits and interpersonal connections. And nonprofits can emphasize the importance of collaborative approaches and help bring back humility to philanthropy.

What can nonprofit leaders do?

Nonprofit leaders can help create a more balanced approach to giving by encouraging funders to view nonprofits as partners rather than simply grantees.

They can recognize the power of grantmakers to quickly shift, adapt, and push toward problem solving. They can emphasize the centrality of the human connection and a deeper understanding of partners’ experiences to strengthen their alignment on shared values.

Nonprofits and grantmakers can work together to embed a culture of collaborative funding, power-sharing models between partners and grantees, and a shared terminology reflecting new relationship dynamics.

IMPact: Intentional, Meaningful Philanthropic action

By highlighting the strengths each partner brings to the partnership, nonprofit leaders can advocate for grantmakers to adapt formulas and metrics to cultural context, changing needs, and community-based structures—and to lead with IMPact: Intentional, Meaningful Philanthropic action. IMPact represents a refined version of strategic philanthropy that is created in partnership and grounded in the experience of the community it serves and its changing environments.

Lasting change rarely happens as a linear process, and we can only make progress in partnership if funders are willing to listen, learn, and adapt. These elements can be difficult to quantify, yet they are often the very conditions that make measurable impact possible. And while data matters, so do patience, trust, and a willingness to pursue outcomes that cannot always be measured in the moment. Understanding what matters to each partner is key.

This is particularly true when philanthropy seeks to address complex social challenges. Nonprofits and funders looking to create collective momentum should focus on building trust, communicating thoughtfully, and truly listening to each other. This can lead to greater humility—and, in turn, stronger strategic alignment—throughout the grantmaking process.

With values and beliefs driving 68% of giving, and some 90% of donors looking for personal fulfillment through their philanthropy, it has become challenging to align the deeply personal with the strategically impactful and measurable. This challenge presents an opportunity for nonprofits who, through their partnership, can create IMPact.

Accountability matters. Measurement matters. Evidence matters. But philanthropy’s most important contributions are achieved through the values it shares with its nonprofit partners: trust, belonging, opportunity, flexibility, and responsiveness. These form the foundation upon which lasting social change is built.

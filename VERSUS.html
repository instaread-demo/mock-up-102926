    Bone music: How X-rays kept music alive in the Soviet Union

    The fascinating story of how ingenuity circumvented a ban on popular music in the USSR.

    The story of how X-rays were used to circumvent a ban on popular music in the Soviet Union is a great example of how technological innovation and passion can overcome adversity.

    The Iron Curtain that divided East from West after the Second World War was an ideological barrier that Joseph Stalin and his comrades used to keep the Soviet Union ‘uncontaminated’ by Western culture.

    In its bid to embed socialist ideas into the minds of its citizens, the Soviet government cracked down on any music it deemed counter-revolutionary. Unsurprisingly, the list of forbidden music included Rock and Roll, Blues, and Jazz. 

    A lot of early and contemporary Russian music made the banned list too. Folk songs composed in the Gulags, or music by Russian émigrés who chose not to return to the motherland after the war, were all off-limits. What if you were a fan of the Tango or Foxtrot? Banned. The licentious dance moves those genres encouraged would surely corrupt the morals of the Soviet youth.

    In 1946, a Polish man called Stanislaw Philo arrived in Leningrad with something that would disrupt the Soviet government’s iron grip on popular music. Like so many other soldiers, Philo returned from Germany after WW2 carrying a war trophy. 

    While some Soviet soldiers came back with watches, flags, or German bayonets as spoils of war, Philo arrived in Leningrad carrying a Telefunken recording lathe. The recording lathe, or disk cutter, was designed to be a portable recording device for war correspondents to use in the field.

    A recording lathe is like a gramophone in reverse. Instead of a needle that bumps up and down as it follows the grooves of a record, it has a cutting head that turns an audio input into grooves cut into a blank plastic disk.

    Philo got permission to open a shop on Nevsky Prospekt, Leningrad’s high street. There he set up his lathe and charged people a few rubles to make a recording of their voice as a souvenir. But it was after hours that his recording lathe became a real money spinner.

    Once the doors to his shop were shut at night, Philo used his recording lathe to make copies of banned gramophone records. He sold these bootleg disks to music lovers, including a man called Ruslan Bogoslowski.

    Bogoslowski became a regular at Philo’s shop and eventually met another fan of Philo’s bootleg recordings, Boris Taigin. Bogoslowski and Taigin bonded over their love for music and talked about how great it would be if they had their own recording lathe.

    Bogoslowski had secretly been making detailed drawings of Philo’s Telefunken device and was convinced that he could recreate it. They headed off to Bogoslowski’s father’s workshop and cobbled together bits and pieces of gramophones and other tools to make their own recording lathe.

    The DIY device worked well, but there was a problem. They needed blank vinyl, shellac, or lacquer disks to record the music, but the Soviet government controlled the supply of these. You couldn’t just pop into a shop and buy a pack of blank disks.

    Fortunately for the novice bootleggers, another unnamed music lover had a lightbulb moment and realized that X-ray plates could be used instead of purpose-made blank disks. And if you wanted used X-ray plates, they were literally giving them away.

    The Soviet government mandated that hospitals discard their X-rays after a year because they were a fire risk. Early X-ray films were made from cellulose nitrate which degrades easily and is highly flammable and potentially explosive.

    If you wanted a stack of X-rays to make your bootleg records, you just needed to dig around in dumpsters behind a hospital or trade a bottle of vodka and a few rubles with a hospital orderly.

    The X-rays were roughly cut into 7” disks and the center hole was burned into the disc using a cigarette. The grooves were cut into the X-rays at 78 rpm and, depending on the machine you used, the quality of the record varied from awful to not bad.

    Because the X-ray records were much thinner than traditional vinyl records, they didn’t last very long. You could play them maybe five or ten times before the grooves wore out.

    The X-Ray Audio Project has digitized some of these recordings to preserve them. Here’s a recording by an unnamed band singing “Big Guitar”. Considering that this was recorded onto X-ray film using a DIY recording device, it’s pretty good.

    b · Big Guitar

    Listening to a recording like this in 1950s Russia must have been an incredible, albeit dangerous, experience.

    The quality of the recordings that Bogoslowski and Taigin made on their device was so good that Philo’s customers soon left him and started to buy their X-ray records. They got so busy that they built additional recording machines and eventually called themselves The Golden Dog Gang.

    These X-ray recordings were known as “bone music”, “music on ribs”, “jazz on bones”, or simply “ribs”. The images of bones on the X-rays added to the cool factor of the bootleg records. The ease with which they could be rolled up and stashed up a shirt sleeve made them easier to smuggle.

    When Bogoslowski shared his recording device design with others, X-ray bootleg operations started to pop up in other areas of Leningrad, eventually spreading to Kyiv, Moscow, and Odesa.

    The Soviet government had eyes and ears everywhere and you can imagine that a sudden demand for used X-rays and a carelessly loud record player soon caught their attention. 

    In 1950, the authorities cracked down on the bone music operations, and The Golden Dog Gang members and other bootleggers were arrested. Bogoslowski and Taigin were sent to the Gulag to serve sentences of five and seven years respectively. Taigin received a longer sentence because he’d been recording his own songs in addition to the bootlegs.

    After Stalin died in 1953, they got an early release and immediately went back to make X-ray bootleg recordings. Bogoslowski was eventually arrested again and served another two years in prison.

    When he got out, Bogoslowski started up his bone music business once more before being arrested for a third time. When he was released after his third and final stint in prison, the political and technological scene had changed. Reel-to-reel machines and relaxed laws had transformed bone music X-ray recordings from a cultural necessity to a historical curiosity.

    In the 1960s, magnetic tape recorders became more accessible in the Soviet Union, so people no longer needed to use recording lathes. They were a lot easier to use and made significantly better recordings.

    In the late 1960s and 1970s, the Soviet government relaxed its stance on certain types of Western music. They realized that trying to completely suppress Western cultural influences was impractical. Eventually, jazz and some pop music that were deemed less politically subversive were played on state-controlled radio stations.

    The government started to produce records of selected Western artists that weren’t considered a threat to Soviet ideology and even encouraged the creation of Soviet versions of Western genres. 

    Even though it was limited and heavily controlled, music lovers no longer needed to dumpster dive for X-rays and risk prison to satisfy their musical appetites.

    Bogoslowski was just one of many bone music entrepreneurs who went to incredible lengths to keep the music playing. Another music enthusiast, Rudy Fuchs, wanted a recording lathe but didn’t have enough cash. He repeatedly donated blood for money until he’d saved enough to buy his own device.

    Fuchs sold blood to buy a machine that recorded banned music on X-rays of the bones of fellow Russians. Bogoslowski and Taigin were willing to risk repeated stints in the Gulag to keep the supply of bone music flowing.

    These were not counter-revolutionaries or political activists. The people making and selling bone music in clandestine black markets and back alleys were music lovers. Innovation and passion combined to overcome repression. X-ray technology was used to heal in a way its inventors could never have imagined.

    When you hold one of the flimsy X-ray disks up to the light, they reveal the broken bones and injuries of anonymous Russians. But when you drop a needle onto the spinning disk and turn up the volume, you get a glimpse into the minds of an irrepressible group of Russians who refused to be denied the music they loved.

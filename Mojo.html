Bob Dylan’s Rolling Thunder Revue: “I was frightened he might get killed and dumped in the bayou…”

Alligator stew, knife attacks and marital recriminations, inside the madness of Dylan’s legendarily chaotic travelling circus.

@Ken Regan
by Ray Padgett |

As Bob Dylan returns to UK shores once more and a new box set of his extraordinary 1976 live shows is unveiled, in the latest issue of MOJO – available to order now – we explore the tours that sealed Dylan’s legend. In this extract from our cover feature, Ray Padgett takes a trip back to the second leg of the Rolling Thunder revue, a tour beset by ill omens: not least Dylan’s failing marriage. However, as the new 36-disc set makes clear, bad vibes were nightly transformed into incendiary performances and unpredictable reinventions…

Early May, 1976. Deep in the Louisiana swamps, the musicians of Bob Dylan’s Rolling Thunder Revue are spending the afternoon with Bobby Charles, the cult hero who’d made an album with The Band in 1972, and who also, in a previous life, had written See You Later, Alligator. On the road down South for the second Rolling Thunder tour, a show has been cancelled and Charles – whom Dylan later calls “one of the most melodious voices ever transmuted into a piece of vinyl” — has invited everyone to his place in the bayou for a barbecue. But he isn’t cooking hamburgers.

According to percussionist Gary Burke, not long before his passing earlier this year, “There were a lot of local characters and this unusual-looking stew. I said, What is this? This guy with a Bowie knife in his belt, somebody you don’t want to mess with in a dark alley, says, ‘It’s alligator. I caught this last night.’”

“There were no plates or utensils or anything,” says Roger McGuinn, one of the tour’s featured performers. “You go and take a handful of alligator, put it in your mouth, and chase it with a beer.”

Everyone stays over, eating alligator, drinking beer and mezcal. Meanwhile, Charles strums his guitar and spins records by 1950s Cajun band the Louisiana Aces. Every time anyone says something memorable, Charles drawls, in his thick Louisiana accent, “Pencil and paper… pencil and paper,” then scrawls it down as a possible future song title.

The guy who caught the alligator calls himself Bird, and he doesn’t take kindly to all these hippie musicians coming into his swamp. Suzi Ronson, partner to ex-Bowie, now-Dylan guitarist Mick Ronson, remembers: “They were gambling, and the guy threw this enormous knife. It whizzed right past Mick’s face and stuck in the thing behind him. Then he picked up his gun and laid it across the table. I was frightened that Mick might get killed and dumped in the bayou. Bob Neuwirth rushed in and took Mick outside.”

David Mansfield, the baby-faced 19-year-old who played steel guitar, mandolin, and violin, calls it “a lost weekend” – one of many such moments of chaos on the second Rolling Thunder tour.

Rolling Thunder 1976, it quickly became clear, was not at all like Rolling Thunder 1975. The traveling gypsy carnival spirit of Dylan’s autumn jaunt had departed and Dylan’s mood had plummeted. His marriage to Sara Dylan, who appeared occasionally on the tour but mostly kept her distance, appeared in jeopardy; they would formally divorce the following spring.

Desire had been released in January, but many of the album’s songs, which had featured so prominently on the autumn dates, had been jettisoned. Instead, Rolling Thunder 1976 became the Blood On The Tracks tour that had never happened. Idiot Wind, If You See Her, Say Hello, and Shelter From The Storm all made their live debuts in cutting new arrangements. Dylan wrote those songs when his marriage began to struggle. Now, as it collapsed completely, they got angrier.

“That Idiot Wind, oh my god, that’s one of the most impassioned vocals I ever heard,” says bassist and bandleader Rob Stoner of one of the nightly highlights. “And there’s Sara sitting right in front while he’s singing this shit. It was some incendiary emotional stuff to witness.”

As a massive new box set – The Rolling Thunder Revue: The 1976 Live Recordings – makes clear, dour offstage vibes translated to incredible onstage music. The nine-track Hard Rain live album, released in September 1976, barely scratched the surface. Across 39 discs, fans can now hear almost every 1976 show, some never heard before even by the most dedicated bootleg collector. The set makes a powerful case for a tour that some Dylan fans have given short shrift.

There was an “edgy punk energy” to Dylan’s new sound, says Gary Burke: songs reinvented in loud and aggressive new ways, sometimes screamed at top volume. “It was more wild and uncontained,” adds violinist Scarlet Rivera, a dominant presence with Dylan both musically and visually ever since her playing on Desire. “It felt like two different tours.”

“Oh my god, we’re gonna electrocute Bob!”

bid defiance

September 29, 2026 6:49 AM

B

bid defiance (idiom)
/bɪd dɪˈfaɪəns/

Meaning

  • To stand your ground and refuse to back down.
  • To openly resist someone or something that tries to push you around.
  • To challenge a rule, threat, or force instead of giving in.
  • To treat danger or opposition as if it does not scare you.

Synonyms: defy, resist, stand firm, refuse to yield, challenge, brave, flout, set at naught.

Example Sentences

  1. The small town bid defiance to the new tax and simply refused to collect it.
  2. Mike bid defiance to the storm and kept walking until he reached the cabin.
  3. Sarah bid defiance to the critics and released the film exactly the way she wanted it.
  4. Wrapped in warm coats, they bid defiance to the frost and stayed outside until midnight.

Etymology and Origin

Two Words That Already Carried Force

The phrase did not arrive as a sudden invention. It grew from two older English habits. Bid here is the old verb that means to offer or to announce something in a formal way. Defiance came into English from French and already meant a challenge, a breaking of trust, or a blunt refusal to submit. Put side by side, the two words said more than “I disagree.” They said a person was standing up and making that resistance public.

From a Challenge of Arms to a Stance of Resistance

English writers had long used bid with the language of war. A man could bid battle or bid arms, meaning he was offering a fight. That older pattern sits behind the later phrase. Bid defiance keeps the same ceremonial feel. The speaker is not muttering in private. He is declaring that he will not yield. Over time the target changed. People still used it against an enemy, but they also used it against weather, illness, a harsh law, or an idea they would not accept.

England as the First Home of the Phrase

The phrase first turns up in English print, in English books, and in English arguments. That is the safest conclusion from the surviving record. Sixteenth-century England was full of sermons, pamphlets, and public disputes, and writers needed a short line that sounded firm without sounding crude. This one did the work.

The Earliest Printed Record

The earliest known printed use comes from Lewis Evans and his book The Castle of Christianitie, printed in London in 1568 by Henry Denham. In the address to his friends, Evans says that duty to God, loyalty to the Queen, and love of country:

“do in dede now compell me… to bid defiance vnto Idolatrie.”

He is not talking about a battlefield. He is saying, in public, that he will not accept what he sees as false worship. That sentence is the first solid printed record of the phrase.

How Later Writers Kept the Formula Alive

Once the wording was in print, later writers reached for it when they needed a grave kind of resistance. In 1629 Philip Massinger put it into The Roman Actor, where the speakers “bid defiance, To them, and fate.” Much later, Thomas Babington Macaulay used the older past tense when he wrote of a spirit that “had bidden defiance to… the House of Valois.” These lines did not invent a new meaning. They simply carried the same idea from religion into drama and history.

A Political Afterlife That Outlived the Stage

The phrase found a second life in politics. In March 1763, speaking on the Excise Bill, William Pitt the Elder said that “the poorest man may in his cottage bid defiance to all the forces of the Crown.” Wind and rain might enter the ruined house, he added, but the king himself might not. That image stuck. Later speakers and judges used it when they wanted to say that a private home, however poor, still had a right to stand firm.

Why the Phrase Still Sounds Older Than Everyday Speech

One small fact makes the phrase interesting. English kept bid defiance while letting sister phrases such as bid battle and bid arms fade away. The wording never became everyday talk, and it never quite disappeared either. It survives in formal writing and in historical speech as a short, slightly ceremonial way of saying that someone has chosen not to yield and is willing to say so out loud.
